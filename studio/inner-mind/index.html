Throughblack glass

A language model is glass in one sense: every number inside it can be printed and checked. It is black in another: from those numbers, no one can yet read most of what it is doing. This is a film and a field guide to an AI agent, the network inside it, and the questions science hasn’t closed.

01AgentA model in a loop

An agent is a model in a loop

Ask an AI agent to fix a bug and it seems to work like a person. It looks around, tries something, checks the result and tries again. Underneath, one operation repeats.

The model is handed a transcript: its instructions, the task, and everything that has happened so far. It writes what should come next. When that text asks for a tool, such as running a command, opening a file or searching the web, the program around the model carries out the request and pastes the result into the transcript. Then the model is called again on the longer transcript. That loop is the architecture of most agents.

The plan, the tool calls and the final answer are all text the model writes. Within a task, the agent’s memory is mostly the transcript itself, held in a context window that can run to hundreds of thousands of tokens. When the window fills, agents summarise older material or write notes to files they can read back later. Larger jobs are split up. An orchestrating agent hands parts of the work to sub-agents, each with a fresh context, and reads back their summaries.

Models are trained for this work too, with reinforcement learning on long tasks whose results can be checked, such as code that has to pass its tests. Still, nothing in the loop is a separate planner or a stored goal. Planning, persistence and recovery from mistakes all have to come out of a network predicting text. That is why the inside of the network matters.

OpenHow far ahead a model plans within a single step, and how reliably it holds a goal across hundreds of steps, are active research questions.

02TokensText becomes numbers

Text becomes numbers

A language model never sees letters. Text is first cut into tokens: whole common words, pieces of rarer ones, punctuation and spaces.

In English a token averages about three-quarters of a word. “ the”, with its leading space, is one token. A rare name, a long technical word or a word from a less common language may take several. The vocabulary is fixed before training, usually by byte-pair encoding: start from single bytes and repeatedly merge whichever pair appears most often in a large sample of text, until the vocabulary reaches its target size.

Each token then becomes a plain integer, its row number in that vocabulary. Everything the model does starts from those integers.

The cuts explain some well-known blind spots. A model that receives “ strawberry” as one or two opaque chunks has no direct view of the letters inside, which is part of why models long struggled to count its r’s.

03MeaningA space of directions

Each token becomes a point in a vast space

A token’s number picks out one row of a large table, the embedding matrix. That row is a list of numbers, and the list works like a set of coordinates.

GPT-3 uses 12,288 numbers per token, so every token is a point in a space with 12,288 dimensions. Nobody assigns the coordinates. They start random and are adjusted throughout training, and they end up organised by use: words that appear in similar contexts land near each other.

Some relationships become directions. The step from a country to its capital, or from a word to its plural, points roughly the same way wherever it starts. Word-embedding models such as word2vec showed this clearly (Mikolov et al., 2013). The broader claim, that models represent concepts as directions, is called the linear representation hypothesis. It accounts for much of what interpretability has found, but not everything: days of the week and months of the year turn out to be arranged in circles (Engels et al., 2024).

Word order enters separately. Many current models rotate the vectors used inside attention by an angle that depends on each token’s position, so “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” produce different results.

04AttentionTokens consult each other

Attention lets tokens consult each other

On its own, the token “ is” means almost nothing. Attention moves information between positions, so each token can take in what came before it.

At every position, each attention head makes three vectors from the token’s current state: a query, which says what it is looking for; a key, which says what it contains; and a value, which is what it will pass on. The query is compared with the key of every earlier position. A softmax turns those scores into weights that add up to one, and the token receives a weighted blend of the earlier values. A head can only look backwards, which is what lets a model generate text one token at a time.

Each layer runs many heads side by side, and each learns its own kind of question. The design comes from the 2017 transformer paper (Vaswani et al.). GPT-3 runs 96 heads in each of its 96 layers, 9,216 in all.

A few kinds of head are understood completely. A previous-token head copies information forward by one place. An induction head finds an earlier occurrence of the current token and looks at whatever followed it: having once read “Golden Gate”, the next time it sees “Golden” it predicts “Gate”. Induction heads form abruptly during training, at the same point that models get much better at learning from examples in their prompt (Olsson et al., 2022). Most heads in a large model have no tidy description.

05StreamLayer by layer

Dozens of layers write into one running sum

A transformer is a tall stack of layers: 96 in GPT-3, and from dozens to more than a hundred in large models today. Each token position carries a vector up through the whole stack.

Researchers call this the residual stream. A layer doesn’t replace the vector. It reads from it, computes something and adds the result back in, so the stream works like a shared notebook that every attention head and every MLP block can read and write. Because everything is added into the same space, the stream carries many pieces of information at once, written by different layers in overlapping directions.

In 2025 Anthropic researchers traced how Claude 3.5 Haiku completes “Fact: the capital of the state containing Dallas is”. Features representing Dallas activated features representing Texas. Texas, together with features signalling that a capital was wanted, pushed the output towards Austin. A second route ran from Dallas straight to Austin as a shortcut. When the researchers replaced the Texas features with California features, the model answered Sacramento (Lindsey et al., 2025).

That intervention is the important step. A description of a circuit is only trusted if changing the circuit changes the behaviour the way the description predicts.

06NeuronsA weighted sum and a bend

A neuron is a weighted sum and a bend

Between the attention layers sit MLP blocks. In a standard transformer they hold about two-thirds of the weights.

An MLP block widens each token’s vector to about four times its size, applies a simple non-linear function to every element, and narrows it back. Each element of the wide middle layer is an artificial neuron. It multiplies its inputs by learned weights, adds them up with a bias, and passes the total through a curve that bends near zero, such as ReLU or GELU.

The bend is what gives a network its power. Without it, any stack of layers collapses into a single matrix multiplication and can only separate data with straight lines. With it, a large enough network can approximate essentially any continuous function, as Cybenko and others proved in 1989. The proof says nothing about how to find the right weights, or how many neurons the job will take.

MLP blocks seem to act partly as memory. Particular neurons respond to particular input patterns and push up particular next tokens (Geva et al., 2021), and editing a few middle-layer weights can change a stored fact, such as the city the Eiffel Tower is in (Meng et al., 2022). Later work found that where a fact appears to be stored is a poor guide to where it is best edited (Hase et al., 2023). The tidy picture of facts filed in drawers is incomplete.

07PredictionOne token at a time

It writes one token at a time

At the top of the stack, the vector at the last position is compared with every token in the vocabulary. The result is one score per token.

A softmax turns the scores into probabilities. One token is drawn at random according to them, added to the text, and the whole stack runs again to choose the next. A long answer is thousands of these draws, each one conditioned on all the text before it.

Temperature shapes the draw. Dividing the scores by a temperature below 1 sharpens the distribution towards the favourite. A temperature above 1 flattens it and gives the long tail of unlikely tokens a real chance. Many systems also cut the tail off before drawing, keeping only the most likely tokens that together reach a set probability (top-p sampling).

Writing one token at a time doesn’t mean thinking only one token ahead. When Claude 3.5 Haiku writes a rhyming couplet, features for candidate rhyme words become active before it starts the second line, and the line is written towards them. When researchers suppressed the planned word “rabbit”, the model ended the line on “habit” instead (Lindsey et al., 2025).

08TrainingNobody wrote the rules

Nobody wrote the rules

Everything described so far was learned. Training starts from random weights.

The model reads a stretch of text, predicts each next token, and is scored on how much probability it gave to the token that actually came next. Backpropagation, an application of the chain rule from calculus (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986), works out for every weight how a small change would have improved that score. Every weight then moves a tiny step in the helpful direction. This is gradient descent. A large run repeats it hundreds of thousands of times over trillions of tokens: GPT-3 trained on about 300 billion tokens in 2020, and Llama 3 on more than 15 trillion in 2024.

Assistant behaviour comes from further training: on examples of good answers, on preference ratings from people and from other models, and with reinforcement learning on tasks whose outcomes can be checked.

Engineers choose the architecture, the data and the objective. They don’t choose how the finished network solves any particular problem. Those solutions are found by the optimisation rather than designed, so they have to be studied afterwards, the way a biologist studies an organism.

09SuperpositionMore ideas than neurons

There are more ideas than neurons

If each neuron stood for one idea, reading a model would be easy: label the neurons. Most neurons don’t work that way.

Neurons in language models are usually polysemantic. One neuron in a small model studied by Anthropic responded to academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests and Korean text (Bricken et al., 2023). The leading explanation is superposition. A model needs to represent far more features than it has neurons, and most features are rare, so it stores them as directions that are almost, but not quite, at right angles, and tolerates a little interference. In toy models trained to do this, the features settle into regular shapes, such as five features arranged as a pentagon in two dimensions (Elhage et al., 2022).

To pull features back out, researchers train a second network, a sparse autoencoder, that rewrites the model’s activity as a combination of many more directions, only a few active at once. Applied to Claude 3 Sonnet, it produced up to 34 million features (Templeton et al., 2024). Many are readable: the Golden Gate Bridge, errors in code, inner conflict, sycophantic praise. Turning up the Golden Gate Bridge feature made the model claim to be the bridge.

Whether features are the model’s real units of thought is unresolved. Autoencoders never reconstruct the activity perfectly, and what they miss may hold much of the computation. In 2025 Google DeepMind’s interpretability team reported that autoencoder features did worse than simple linear probes at detecting harmful intent, and shifted its effort elsewhere.

What no one can yet explain

None of this is magic. Every step of the computation is ordinary arithmetic, performed exactly, and anyone with the weights can repeat it. The difficulty is understanding.

A worm with 302 neurons and a known wiring diagram still keeps secrets. A frontier language model has billions of weights. Interpretability research is moving fast, and in January 2026 MIT Technology Review named it one of the year’s ten breakthrough technologies. Its practitioners are also the first to state its limits. Anthropic’s circuit-tracing method gave a satisfying explanation for about a quarter of the prompts its authors tried, and even then captured only part of the computation (Ameisen et al., 2025). These are the largest open questions.

  • What is a feature?

    Sparse autoencoders turn a model’s activity into millions of readable features, but they never reconstruct it exactly. Autoencoders trained on the same model and data, differing only in their random start, find different sets of features (Paulo and Belrose, 2025).

    OpenDoes a model have a true set of features, and how much computation hides in what autoencoders miss?

    Templeton et al. 2024 · Paulo & Belrose 2025

  • Why does scale work?

    Make a model bigger and give it more data and computing power, and its loss falls along a smooth power law across many orders of magnitude (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). The trend is reliable enough to plan billion-dollar training runs around.

    OpenWhy a power law, and why these exponents? Several theories compete, and none is settled.

    Kaplan et al. 2020 · Hoffmann et al. 2022

  • Emergence

    Some abilities, such as multi-step arithmetic, seem absent in smaller models and then appear suddenly at a certain scale (Wei et al., 2022). Others argue that the jumps come partly from all-or-nothing scoring, and that the underlying progress is smooth (Schaeffer et al., 2023).

    OpenWhich abilities will the next, larger model gain? No one can reliably say in advance.

    Wei et al. 2022 · Schaeffer et al. 2023

  • Why does it generalise?

    Classical statistics says a model with more parameters than examples should memorise its data and fail on anything new. Networks can memorise: they will learn completely random labels (Zhang et al., 2017). Yet on real data they generalise, and test error can fall, rise and fall again as they grow (Belkin et al., 2019; Nakkiran et al., 2019).

    OpenWhat in the data, the architecture or the training steers networks towards solutions that generalise?

    Zhang et al. 2017 · Belkin et al. 2019 · Nakkiran et al. 2019

  • Grokking

    Small networks trained on modular arithmetic first memorise their examples, with no skill on new ones. Then, long afterwards, test accuracy jumps to perfect (Power et al., 2022). Reverse-engineering one such network showed it had learned to add using rotations and trigonometric identities (Nanda et al., 2023).

    OpenDo large models pass through hidden transitions like this, and could we see them coming?

    Power et al. 2022 · Nanda et al. 2023

  • Learning from the prompt

    Show a model a few examples of a new pattern and it continues the pattern, with no change to its weights. Induction heads explain part of this (Olsson et al., 2022). One theory holds that attention can carry out something like a step of gradient descent within a single pass (von Oswald et al., 2023).

    OpenHow far does learning in context reach, and is it one mechanism or many?

    Olsson et al. 2022 · von Oswald et al. 2023

  • Does it say what it does?

    Asked to add 36 and 59, Claude 3.5 Haiku runs two paths at once: one estimates the rough size of the answer, the other works out the last digit exactly. Asked how it did it, the model describes carrying the one (Lindsey et al., 2025). Given hints, Claude 3.7 Sonnet often used them without saying so, mentioning them only about a quarter of the time (Chen et al., 2025).

    OpenWhen can a model’s written reasoning be trusted as a record of what it computed?

    Lindsey et al. 2025 · Chen et al. 2025

  • Where do falsehoods come from?

    Models invent facts fluently. One mechanism has been traced: a default circuit that says it can’t answer, switched off by features that recognise a known entity. When a name seems familiar without being known, the switch can misfire and the model makes something up (Lindsey et al., 2025).

    OpenIs this the main source of invented facts, or one of many?

    Lindsey et al. 2025

  • Can it know itself?

    Researchers injected a concept directly into a model’s activity and asked whether it noticed anything unusual. Claude Opus 4.1 sometimes detected the concept and named it, but only about 20% of the time, even with the best method (Lindsey, 2025). Most of what models say about their own inner states can’t yet be checked.

    OpenCan a model ever be a reliable witness to its own computation?

    Lindsey 2025

11The ledgerAs of October 2026

Known, partly known, open

Known exactly

  • The architecture. Every operation fits in a few hundred lines of code.
  • The arithmetic. Every forward pass can be repeated and inspected, number by number.
  • The objective. What training rewards, and the algorithm that pursues it.
  • The trend. Loss falls smoothly and predictably as models, data and compute grow.
  • Some mechanisms. Induction heads; the indirect-object circuit in GPT-2 small, 26 heads in 7 classes (Wang et al., 2022); modular addition by rotation.

Partly understood

  • Representation. Concepts as directions, mostly, packed in superposition.
  • Memory. Facts are stored and recalled largely through MLP blocks.
  • Learning in context. Part of it runs through induction heads.
  • Reasoning and planning. Seen step by step in traced examples.
  • Readable by design. Models trained with most weights fixed at zero have circuits small enough to draw, at some cost in capability (Gao et al., 2025).

Open

  • A complete account of how any frontier model produces any long answer.
  • Why scaling follows power laws, with these exponents.
  • Which abilities the next, larger model will have.
  • Whether a model’s written reasoning reflects its computation.
  • How to verify a model’s goals or values from its weights.
  • Whether understanding can keep pace with the models it studies.
